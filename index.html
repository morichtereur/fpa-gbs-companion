The argument in one pass

FP&A is moving into GBS along a predictable path: scale opens the door, maturity widens the mandate, and data readiness decides who converts automation into intelligence.

1

Adoption is a function of scale, and the direction is settled. GBS delivery rises step for step with FP&A size to 100% above 2,000 FTE — and 85% of today’s non-adopters plan to follow within 1–3 years.

Exhibit 1

Adoption rises step for step with scale — above 2,000 FP&A FTE it is universal

Share of companies delivering FP&A through GBS, % · by FP&A organization size

Adoption is not a preference curve — it is a staircase that rises with every size band and ends at 100%. The open question the survey leaves is not whether FP&A moves into GBS, but how far down the size distribution the staircase keeps descending.

So what

For a sub-scale FP&A function the live question is sequencing, not direction: with 85% of non-adopters already planning the move, waiting is a choice about when to standardize — and the study notes most are deliberately sequencing centralization with automation rather than doubting the value.

Exhibit 2

GBS runs the transactional core almost everywhere — the strategic tier opens only with maturity

Share of companies whose GBS supports each process, % · by self-assessed maturity

The study clusters the delivered scope into three plateaus — and the gradient is the finding: what companies hand to GBS first is the repetitive core, while planning arrives second and the strategic tier arrives only with maturity.

Strategic Planning is the sharpest cut: 0% of basic companies hand it to GBS, 17% of developing ones — and 75% of mature ones.

So what

A GBS mandate is earned in tiers: delivering the transactional core credibly is the entry ticket to planning work, and planning is the proving ground for the strategic tier. The exhibit reads left to right as the function’s career path — skipping a tier is what the trust data in Exhibit 4 punishes.

Exhibit 3

The planned expansion concentrates on planning and forecasting — the strategic tier stays retained

Share of companies, % · current delivery plus planned addition within 1–3 years

Stack what is delivered today against what companies plan to add, and the next one to three years concentrate on exactly the processes the study calls the way out of the “extended workbench”: Budgeting +24pt and Forecasting +24pt — each planned to nearly double — alongside Performance Reporting’s +35pt. The strategic tier, by contrast, is barely on the roadmap.

So what

The next one to three years decide whether planning becomes a GBS process or stays a local craft. The +3pt on M&A support says the barrier at the top is not capability but ownership — which is why the study treats stakeholder trust as a managed workstream, not a communications afterthought.

Exhibit 4

The binding constraint rotates with maturity: first scope, then trust, then data

Share of companies naming each challenge, % · by self-assessed maturity

Read the challenge chart by maturity level and the barriers change identity as a function matures. Basic setups are stopped by scope and silos (both 100%). Developing ones hit stakeholder buy-in (62%) — the trust problem that appears precisely when GBS reaches for judgment work. For mature organizations no single blocker dominates: silos, buy-in, tools and data quality all settle at 50%, which is what remains when structure is solved.

So what

A transformation plan should target the blocker of the maturity level it is at, not a generic list: secure the mandate first, win the stakeholders second, fix the data third. Spending on the wrong tier’s blocker — data platforms for a basic setup, change communications for a mature one — buys the least movement.

Exhibit 5

Automation is table stakes at every size — embedded AI is where the field separates

Share of companies per size cohort, % · columns sum to 100 · by global FTE

The study’s sharpest sentence — “efficiency is foundational, intelligence is the differentiator” — is visible in the pairing below. Partial automation is the norm in every size cohort. AI is where the cohorts separate: 40% of the smallest companies report no AI or ML use in FP&A at all, against 9% of the largest.

So what

Automation budgets buy parity; data readiness and embedded AI buy advantage — and the study’s point is that the second is gated by the first plus governance, not by tooling. Size still predicts who converts: the largest cohort has both the fewest abstainers (9%) and the most embedded adopters (36%).

The CFO agenda

“Five decisions you can’t delegate nor delay”

The study closes with five leadership choices for the next 24 months. Condensed faithfully:

  1. Revisit the FP&A operating model

    Clear governance, decision rights and service boundaries between retained Finance and GBS — a unified operating backbone with standardized calendars and end-to-end accountability.

    Evidence · silos top every cohort’s list, Exhibit 4

  2. Elevate data readiness to a strategic priority

    Harmonized data models, named data stewards, aligned KPI definitions — data governance treated as a core enabler, not a technical concern.

    Evidence · data is the mature cohort’s residual blocker, Exhibits 4–5

  3. Accelerate automation and AI adoption

    Automate the recurring, rule-based work first; then layer AI onto forecasting, scenario modeling, early-warning indicators and automated insights.

    Evidence · the widening AI gap by size, Exhibit 5

  4. Evolve FP&A roles and skills

    Shift the profile toward interpretation, business partnering, driver-based planning and storytelling — in GBS teams as much as in retained Finance.

    Evidence · the scope shift toward planning work, Exhibits 2–3

  5. Actively manage stakeholder trust

    Transparent service models and regular performance dialogues, so GBS is perceived as a strategic enabler rather than a reporting factory.

    Evidence · buy-in peaks exactly when scope turns strategic, Exhibits 3–4

What this survey can and cannot say

  • ~50 companies, surveyed in H2 2025. Percentages per maturity cohort rest on small groups; positions are meaningful, single points are not.
  • Maturity is self-assessed — and the authors say so themselves, noting that a study about GBS usage “may lead to a certain bias” connecting GBS to maturity. The gradient across cohorts is the robust part.
  • “Supported by GBS” measures presence, not extent. The study states that 100% means a process is generally supported, without specifying how much of it moved.
  • Direction is consistent everywhere. Whatever the individual cell, all five exhibits point the same way: scale enables adoption, maturity widens scope, and automation precedes intelligence.
